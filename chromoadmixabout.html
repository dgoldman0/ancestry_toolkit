About the Pulse & Stability Scores

Human genomes are mosaics of ancestry. Chromosomes are inherited in blocks that get broken down by recombination each generation. Admixture analysis tries to detect what proportion of DNA in a person traces back to different reference populations. Projects like Eurogenes (and other calculators built on ADMIXTURE or similar software) attempt to quantify this.

Traditionally, admixture is summarized as a single proportion per population (e.g. “20% North Atlantic”). But we can also look chromosome by chromosome, which reveals finer structure — a recent migrant ancestor may leave a long block of unusual ancestry on just one chromosome. The drawback is data explosion: instead of one number per population, you now have 22 numbers per population. The Pulse and Stability Scores were designed to distill this into interpretable measures of admixture dynamics.

Why Chromosome-Level Matters

- Recent admixture: produces spikes on a few chromosomes, since recombination has not had time to evenly spread ancestry.
- Ancient or continuous admixture: produces a smoother, more stable distribution across chromosomes.

This is the intuition: Pulse captures short-term “shock” admixture, while Stability captures long-term background mixing.

Weighted Mean and Variance

Chromosomes differ in length. Short ones swing more by chance. We correct for this by weighting by SNP counts (as a proxy for length):

\[ W = \sum_{i=1}^{22} w_i,\quad \mu_w = \frac{\sum_i w_i x_i}{W},\quad \mathrm{Var}_w = \frac{\sum_i w_i(x_i - \mu_w)^2}{W} \]

where \(x_i\) is the percentage for chromosome \(i\), and \(w_i\) is its SNP count.

Adjusted Chromosomal Spike

Sometimes a single chromosome has a very large admixture hit against a given population, even though though there's little to no overlap identified on other chromosomes. To see if one chromosome has an unusually high ancestry proportion, we rescale each chromosome’s deviation by its relative SNP count:

\[ \bar{w} = \frac{1}{22}\sum_i w_i,\quad \Delta_i = (x_i - \mu_w)\sqrt{\tfrac{w_i}{\bar{w}}} \]

Then we define the adjusted maximum and the adjusted spike, which indicates an unusually high admixture in one chromosome against the rest for the population:

\[ \mathrm{Max}_{\text{adj}} = \mu_w + \max_i \Delta_i,\quad R_{\text{adj}} = \frac{\mathrm{Max}_{\text{adj}}}{\mu_w} \]

Theory of the Pulse Metric

The spike value could be used by itself, however the presence of a spike is more meaningful if it likely occurred recently. Suppose an admixture event occurred \(t\) generations ago. Each generation, recombination roughly halves the size of ancestry blocks. That means the chance of finding a large contiguous block (a big spike on one chromosome) decreases by about a factor of two each generation.

To capture this intuition, we divide the adjusted spike ratio by a logarithmic time proxy:

\[ \mathrm{Pulse}_w = \frac{R_{\text{adj}}}{\log_2\!\left(\tfrac{100}{\mu_w}\right)} \]

- The numerator says: how much bigger is the spikiest chromosome than the mean?
- The denominator \(\log_2(100/\mu_w)\) acts as a stand-in for “how many generations ago did this admixture likely occur?” because small overall means correspond to more distant events, and halving per generation naturally gives a log base 2.

Theory of the Stability Metric

Stability measures how smooth the ancestry is distributed across chromosomes. If a population has been present in the lineage for many generations, variation will be low relative to the mean. If it only recently entered, variance will be higher.

\[ \mathrm{Stability}_w = \frac{\mu_w}{\mathrm{Var}_w + 0.1} \times 100 \]

Here the mean is the “signal,” and the variance is the “noise.” The constant 0.1 prevents instability when variance is tiny.

Box–Cox Standardization

Raw values of Pulse and Stability are skewed. To compare across regions, we use the Box–Cox transformation, which finds a power \(\lambda\) that makes the distribution closer to normal, then z-scores:

\[ y_\lambda(x) = \begin{cases} \tfrac{x^\lambda - 1}{\lambda}, & \lambda \neq 0 \\\\ \ln(x), & \lambda = 0 \end{cases} ,\quad z = \frac{y_\lambda(x) - \overline{y_\lambda}}{\mathrm{SD}(y_\lambda)} \]

This yields final standardized Pulse Score and Stability Score.

Uses and Limitations

- Uses: Helps highlight whether a population’s ancestry in an individual reflects a deep stable background (high stability), a sharp pulse-like event (high pulse), or just noise (low both). This can contextualize results from calculators like Eurogenes K36. - Limitations: Reference datasets are imperfect, ancestry proportions are estimates not absolutes, and statistical noise is unavoidable. Chromosome-by-chromosome analysis adds detail but also variability — hence the need for aggregate scores.

In short, Pulse and Stability Scores turn a very large table of per-chromosome admixture values into interpretable signals of ancestry dynamics: was this lineage shaped by deep, continuous mixing, or by a more recent, punctuated pulse?
